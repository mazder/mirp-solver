Your long-term research question can be framed as:

Can quantum or quantum-inspired algorithms provide measurable benefit for computational biomechanics subproblems arising from large-scale patient-specific FEM?

A sensible roadmap is:

Finish and validate the classical MFEM/HPC pipeline first.
Run it across your patient cohort and establish classical baselines: runtime, memory, convergence, accuracy, and scaling.
Identify the true computational bottleneck.
Extract a well-defined subproblem, such as the sparse linear system \(A x=b\), parameter estimation, inverse modeling, reduced-order modeling, or optimization.
Implement the best available classical/HPC method.
Implement a quantum or quantum-inspired counterpart.
Compare accuracy, complexity, runtime, scaling, data-loading cost, and end-to-end usefulness.

For your FEM problem, I would initially rank the quantum research directions roughly as:

1. Quantum linear-system methods — intellectually closest to FEM because every linearized solve ultimately contains \(Ax=b\), but the state-preparation/readout issue is crucial.

2. Quantum-assisted inverse problems / parameter estimation — potentially more practically interesting because you may estimate tumor growth parameters or tissue properties from imaging observations.

3. Quantum optimization — useful if the eventual workflow contains parameter fitting, treatment-planning, mesh/model selection, or large combinatorial search.

4. Quantum reduced-order modeling — potentially very interesting for your ~700-patient cohort because the patient ensemble may admit a much lower-dimensional representation than each individual 8.9-million-DOF FEM system.

The fourth direction may actually become particularly valuable. Instead of asking a quantum computer to solve an 8,874,834-DOF system directly, you might construct a reduced basis from hundreds of patient simulations and investigate quantum methods on the resulting reduced problem. That makes the quantum component much more experimentally manageable while retaining a direct connection to the real biomedical application.

Yes, absolutely. Later we can replace the MFEM-dependent pieces incrementally with your own implementation.

The sensible path would be to keep MFEM as the reference implementation first, then replace components one by one while checking that your results match MFEM. For your current mixed Q2/Q1 solver, the main pieces you would eventually need to own are:

mesh and DOF management
Q2 displacement and Q1 pressure basis functions
quadrature rules
element Jacobians and gradients
element assembly for \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\)
essential boundary-condition elimination
global sparse matrix assembly
true/free DOF mappings
manufactured-solution error integration
MPI partitioning and halo exchange
Krylov solvers and block preconditioners
AMG or some alternative scalable approximation for \(A^{-1}\) and \(M_p^{-1}\)

You do not have to replace all of MFEM at once. A much safer progression is:

Stage 1: keep MFEM mesh/FESpaces, but implement your own element kernels and compare assembled matrices.

Stage 2: implement your own DOF numbering, Q2/Q1 basis evaluation, quadrature, and element assembly.

Stage 3: assemble your own global sparse system and compare \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) numerically against MFEM.

Stage 4: implement your own boundary-condition elimination and reduced system.

Stage 5: use your own FGMRES/block-Schur solver on your matrices.

Stage 6: add MPI/METIS domain decomposition.

Stage 7: move element assembly and eventually selected solver kernels to GPU.

For your BZoneX direction, this is actually a strong architecture:

$$ \text{BZoneX FEM} \rightarrow \text{own Q2/Q1 assembly} \rightarrow \text{own block-Schur solver} \rightarrow \text{MPI/GPU backend}. $$

MFEM can remain the verification oracle during development. For every replacement, we can check things like

$$ \frac{\|A_{\text{BZoneX}}-A_{\text{MFEM}}\|} {\|A_{\text{MFEM}}\|} $$

and similarly for \(B\), \(C\), the RHS, and the final solution.

So yes: the MFEM portion we are using now is implementable ourselves later. I would not do it before we finish validating the current solver, though, because MFEM gives us a trustworthy reference while we establish the mathematics. After the patient solver is working, replacing MFEM piece-by-piece becomes much lower risk.
